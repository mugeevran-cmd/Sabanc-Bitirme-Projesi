Direction of travel matters more than level here: the five-day average has fallen to **+0.013** against a twenty-day baseline of **+0.056**, a shift of **−0.042**. Sentiment has not collapsed. It has drained.

Dense and lexical retrieval agreed completely, which is what happens when a corpus is this dominated by a single entity — `SP500` leads the drivers with 8 mentions, `RALLY` follows with 3, and `EARNINGS`, `JPM` and `GS` appear once each.

What the coverage actually argues about is whether the rally has gone too far, and it does not settle the question. One headline manages to hold both positions at once: *"The S&P 500 is overbought, but that doesn't mean it's time to sell"* (2024-02-29). This is genuine information — named institutions making specific valuation claims — rather than crowd reaction. But it is information that cancels, and a committee cannot trade a tie.

The one reading that needs explaining rather than reporting is the gap between two of our own numbers. The Market Fear & Greed index stands at **88.3/100**, firmly in the greed band, while today's headlines score **−0.161**. There is no contradiction, because the two measure different windows: the index is a twenty-day smoothed percentile ranked against everything known up to this date, and today is a single session. Positioning is still complacent; the news flow has already turned. That is what the beginning of a turn looks like, and it is precisely why the greed reading must not be taken as confirmation of the rally.

Coverage volume runs **+1.4 sigma** above its sixty-day norm without crossing the crowded-attention threshold, and at 31 headlines this is a real measurement rather than a handful. Nor does the negative share come close to the level at which one-sided coverage becomes a contrarian signal in its own right. The attention is elevated for the obvious reason: an index at record highs invites people to ask whether it deserves them.
